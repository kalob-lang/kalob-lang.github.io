Phonology & Orthography

The sounds of Kalob are governed by strict phonotactics and an expanded 7-vowel system that drives the language's grammar.

📢 The 7-Vowel Engine

Kalob expands the standard 5-vowel system. These vowels are not just phonetic; they are grammatical indicators for tense, case, and function.

Grammatical Functions

a Singular, Future Tense, Duty Adj/Adv, "Least"
e Plural, Past Tense, General Adj/Adv, "More"
i Prepositions, Eternal Tense, Eternal Adj/Adv, "Most"
o Prepositions, Present Tense, Transitory Adj/Adv, "Less"
v Conjunctions, Habitual Tense, Habitual Adj/Adv, "Too"
w Conjunctions, Hypothetical, Possibility Adj/Adv, "Insufficiently"

⚖ Strict Phonotactics

  • ▶
    Hard-to-Soft Flow: Content words strictly enforce a Hard Onset followed by a Soft Coda.
  • ▶
    Central Vowel-Liquid Exclusion: To prevent discernment issues with central vowels 'v' and 'w', no glide of 'r' or 'l' is permitted on the coda side when 'v' or 'w' is present just prior (nor may 'r' or 'l' stand alone as a single-consonant coda in content roots).
  • ▶
    Streamlined Clusters: Consonant clusters that cause awkward anaptyxis (forced vowel insertion) are strictly prohibited.

Grammar & Morphology

In Kalob, you can identify the exact grammatical function of a word just by looking at its shape, vowels, and suffixes.

Vocabulary Division

All non-derived words are divided into Shortwords (grammar) and Longwords (content).

Category 1: Shortwords

1 to 4 letters • Grammar/Notions

  • Interjections (1-2): Single vowel for vague notions and two for specific ones.
  • Connectives (2-4):
    • Prepositions (only i, o)
    • Conjunctions (only v, w)
  • Designatives (2-4): Articles/Pronouns. Vowels a (Sg) or e (Pl).
  • Staffwords (2-4): Oral gestures. Must end in u.

Category 2: Longwords

3+ letters • Precise Content

  • Nouns & Numbers (3+): End with a Soft Coda (except zh/j).
  • Verbs (4+): Root + open vowel tense (a, e, i, o, v, w).
  • Attributives (5+): Adjectives/Participles. End in -j. (e.g., -oj, -wj)
  • Modificatives (5+): Adverbs/Gerunds. End in -zh. (e.g., -ozh, -wzh)

Partitive Rule
When "some" or "any" functions strictly as a partitive article—meaning it refers to an unspecified quantity of an uncountable substance—Kalob suppresses it entirely and leaves the noun bare.

For example, "Give some bread and some berries" translates to ed kibho qhidu vd phrvdigu (Give bread and cherries), completely omitting a translation for "some".
2-4 LTRS

Staffwords

"Oral gestures" that govern or bind meaning without attaching directly to the word. They must end in u.

  • nu Indicates negation (not)
  • su/snu Reflexive positive/negative particles
  • khu/khnu Positive/negative interrogatives
  • thu/thnu Positive/negative subordinators
  • lu Definite article ("this very")
    Definite Article
    This staffword acts as a definite article when you need to specify "the very one in question" or "that specific one and no other".

    For instance, while cho ren simply means "there is the man," adding the staffword to make cho lu ren explicitly means "there is the man in question".

Detached Staffwords: Register, Gender & Proclitic Modifiers

Beyond the five core syntactic particles (nu, lu, khu/khnu, su/snu, thu/thnu), Kalob uses detached Staffwords (always ending in -u) as proclitic operators placed immediately before a noun or verb. Because they remain detached rather than fusing to the root, they never distort the phonetic shape of the root word and can be stacked sequentially (e.g., sku ku spige — "counter-mutually spoke").

Relational Precision: Disambiguating Connectives

Natural languages heavily overload prepositions like "for," "by," and "of," forcing listeners to guess the underlying relationship from context. Kalob eliminates prepositional ambiguity through a 16-root directional vector system and dedicated connectives for every logical, spatial, temporal, and causal role.

Disambiguating "For"

pro / plw / po / tri / sho / ci / spi
  • pro ⇒ Exchange or Reason: Transactional exchange, price, or general reason.
    me kibho phlvz pro tom. (I give money for the book.)
  • plw ⇒ Purpose / Intent (Same Subject): "In order to" (Fr. afin de); precedes a verb.
    me merjho plw stwdo. (I travel for study / in order to study.)
  • po ⇒ Enablement / Facilitation (New Subject): "So that / for X to..." (Fr. pour que); also used for proportion.
    me stardo pord po le phlezo. (I open the door for him to enter.)
  • tri ⇒ Benefaction or Devotion: "For the sake/love of."
    me lebo tri pham. (I work for [the sake of] the family.)
  • sho ⇒ Reference or Topic: "As for / concerning / regarding" (Fr. quant à).
    sho tom, ze kargo. (As for the book, it is good.)
  • ci ⇒ Duration: "For [a timeframe] / during" (Fr. pendant).
    me slwmbe ci led. (I slept for [during] the night.)
  • spi ⇒ Precise Destination (vs. Vague to): Heading to an exact destination.
    me libho spi myezd. (I leave for the city • Vague direction: to myezd)

Disambiguating "By"

Case / Prep / Adv
  • al / ale / e-C-a ⇒ Passive Agent (Case): Uses Genitive-Ablative case (ema "by me", al "by a/the", ale "by them").
    tom skribhue al ren. (The book was written by a person.)
  • pi ⇒ Method or Distribution:
    ne phleze phwn pi phwn. (We entered one by one.)
  • pho ⇒ Instrument ("By means of"):
    me starde za pho an shvb. (I opened it by means of a tool.)
  • sri ⇒ Commercial Rate / Proportion ("Percent" = pro khwnd):
    phyad shenge sri cen pro khwnd. (The price rose by 10 percent [10 for 100].)
  • phyo ⇒ Route or Passage ("Via / By way of"):
    ne libhe phyo (au) London. (We left by way of London.)
  • oj ⇒ Proximity ("Near / Beside"):
    me svmo in an pling oj phan. (I live in a house by the river.)
  • imo ⇒ Future Deadline (im + -o):
    me phina imo au Monday. (I will finish by Monday.)
  • lwzh ⇒ Adverbial Idiom ("By chance") • au ⇒ "By the name of":
    me chyane za lwzh. (I spotted it by chance.) • cho an phem au Jill.

The 10-Way Genitive Matrix: Disambiguating "Of"

Semantic Precision

Rather than linking every pair of nouns with a single vague "of," Kalob selects the exact connective or morphological structure that reflects the real-world relationship between the two nouns:

1. Possession (obh)

True ownership, property, or territorial belonging.

pholgu obh Europa
(The countries of Europe)
2. Composition (ti)

The physical material something is made of.

pling ti ston / ring ti chin
(House of stone / Ring of gold)
3. Content (ti vs. -inej)

Use ti for a container of X, or -inej to stress "full of X."

an taz ti phan / taz phaninej
(A cup of water / Cup full of water)
4. Origin / Extraction (om / pi)

Coming from a place (om) or produced by (pi).

ribh om kend
(The arrival from [of] town)
5. Kinship / Lineage (al / ale)

Family lineage or birth (al Sg. / ale Pl. possessor).

tu kind al rwghambozd
(The daughter of the king)
6. Epexegetical (klv)

Renames or specifies the head noun ("namely / that is").

an kibh klv manjh
(A gift of [namely] food)
7. Qualification (-ej)

Innate quality; converts the second noun into an adjective.

an parjh rwghambozdej
(A morsel of royalty / royal morsel)
8. Regional / Brand Origin

Direct proper-noun apposition (optional au in speech).

pherm (au) Shanpanyw
(Wine of Champagne)
9. Partitive ([Suppressed])

Unspecified "some of" an uncountable noun is omitted.

ed kibho qhid.
(Give [some of the] bread.)
10. Resolving the Subjective vs. Objective Genitive

English action-nouns like "the love of God" are ambiguous. Kalob resolves the vector explicitly:

Subjective (Agent = al): lwbh al tibh (God's love [by God])
Objective (Target = lo / ijh / ad): lwbh lo tibh (Love towards God)

Personal Pronouns: The Consonant Core

For almost all other words in the designative class (articles, demonstratives, relatives, interrogatives, and indefinites), the rule is absolute: a final -a indicates singular, and -e indicates plural. However, personal pronouns operate on a completely different mechanical system. The grammatical concepts of person, gender, and number are encoded entirely within a single characteristic consonant: m (1 Sg.), d (2 Fam. Sg.), bh (2 Res. Sg.), l (3 Anim. Sg.), z (3 Inan. Sg.), zh (3 Abs. Sg.), n (1 Pl.), b (2 Fam. Pl.), g (2 Res. Pl.), dh (3 Anim. Pl.), j (3 Inan. Pl.), jh (3 Abs. Pl.). Vowels are then wrapped around this consonant to assign the case.

Abstract Pronoun Rule

The Abstract pronoun serves as the primary tool for referencing non-physical entities. Its usage is strictly enforced in two specific contexts:

  1. Conceptual Antecedents: When the referent is a verb, a clause, or an intangible concept (e.g., "The theory is complex; it [Abstract] requires study").
  2. Pleonastic Subjects: Kalob maintains a rigid SVO architecture. In instances where a verb lacks a logical "doer"—such as weather phenomena—the Abstract pronoun is utilized as a structural anchor.
Example: Weather
  • En: "It is raining."
  • Ka: zhe shvbho (It rains)

By doing this, the language satisfies the requirement for a subject slot without erroneously implying that a physical object or animate agent is performing the action, keeping the syntax tree logically consistent.

Definiteness & The Designative Matrix

Kalob does not rely on a standard definite article like the English "the." Instead, definiteness, spatial proximity, epistemic certainty, and quantification are governed by a precise system of bare nouns, the staffword lu, and the Designative class—which strictly encodes singulars (or collective unities) with a and plurals with e.

The Four Tiers of Definiteness Core Rule

1. Default Definite (Bare Noun)

A bare noun is inherently definite ("the") or refers to the general class as a whole.

merjherd = the traveler
merjherdu = the travelers
2. Hyper-Definite (lu)

The staffword lu specifies "this very one" or "the exact one in question."

lu merjherd = this very traveler
cho lu ren = there is the man in question
3. Indefinite (an / ane)

Marks an unspecified countable individual (an) or plural group (ane).

an merjherd = a traveler
ane merjherdu = some travelers
4. Zero-Partitive & Obliques

Uncountable partitive "some" is omitted. Oblique articles fuse case directly:

ed kibho qhidu = give [some] bread
al / ale (of a/them) • ad / ade (to a/some)

Disambiguating "All", "Each" & "Every" aj / the / spe / spa

Kalob strictly separates universal quantifiers by whether they refer to people vs. things, or view a group collectively vs. individually:

  • aj / aje (Collective Universality): "All/every" as a unified group without distinguishing people or things.
    phwn pro aje, aje pro phwn (One for all, all for one.)
  • the (All Things): Strictly for objects and concepts in general ("everything / all things").
    the moghi. (Everything dies.)
  • spe (Everybody): Strictly for people as a collective whole (Fr. tout le monde).
    lib pro spe. (Liberty for all [everyone].)
  • spa (Distributive "Each / Every"): Singles out individuals or units one by one.
    spa sad khabho zae kladu. (Every age has its pleasures.) • spa threz silbhu (Three dollars each.)

Distributive vs. Moral "Others" abh / abhe vs. tra

abh (Sg.) & abhe (Pl.) — General & Distributive

Means "another," "the other," or "the others" for both people and things. Can stand alone as a pronoun or modify a noun:

  • abhe sadu, abhe qondu (Other times, other customs)
  • abh tag (The other day) • abh rw me (Another than me)
tra — Collective & Human ("Other People")

Strictly a pronoun referring to other people in general in a societal or moral context (Fr. autrui). Because it represents humanity as a collective unity, it keeps the singular -a ending and inherently absorbs prepositions ("of/to/unto others"):

ed nu phajhi tra... (Do not do unto others...)

Zero Count vs. Zero Mass ("No / None") sya vs. nya

English uses "no" or "none" for everything, but Kalob mathematically separates discrete countable items from uncountable mass quantities:

sya ⇒ Countable Items

Means "not a single one / zero-count." Used with countable nouns:

zhe svbho sya renu.
There are no people.
me khabho sya kindu.
I have no children.
nya ⇒ Uncountable Mass

Means "zero amount of / not any." Used with masses, materials, or abstractions:

ed kibho nya qhid.
Give no [amount of] bread.
me khabho nya phlvz.
I have no money.

Indefinites with Relative Meaning tya / kya / qa / ska

These indefinite designatives inherently bundle an antecedent pronoun with a relative function, allowing them to bridge two clauses without needing a separate relative pronoun:

  • tya (Exhaustive Totality — "All that / Everything that"): Refers to the entirety of an action, possession, or statement (Fr. tout ce qui / tout ce qu'on). While kra is "something" and the is "all things in general," tya is an exhaustive relative designative.
  • kya / kye (Universal Personal — "Whoever / Whosoever"): Refers strictly to human beings ("anyone who / no matter which person"; Pl. "whichever people").
  • qa / qe (Universal Inanimate — "Whatever / Whatsoever"): The inanimate counterpart to kya. Refers strictly to things, circumstances, or objects ("anything that / no matter what thing"; Pl. "whatever/whichever things") and introduces concessive relative clauses.
  • ska / ske (General Relative — "What one / That which"): Blends the indefinite subject sta ("one / people") with the relative ka/ke ("what") to mean "that which one..." (Sg.) and "those things which / what [pl.]" (ske).
True Relatives (ra / re) & "He Who"
Kalob also has a dedicated set of True Relatives characterized by the consonant r (ra Sg. / re Pl., "who, whom, which, that"), which modify an explicit noun antecedent.

Translation Note on "He who": To translate phrases like "He who", "She who", or "They who", combine a Demonstrative with a True Relative (e.g., le ra... / la ra...) ("they/them who"), ag ra... ("the one who..."), or use pya ra... ("the one of the two who...").

Reflexivity & Reciprocity ("Self" vs. "Each Other")

Kalob strictly separates reflexive actions (acting upon oneself) from reciprocal actions (acting upon each other), and further distinguishes whether reciprocity occurs between a pair of two (pla) or a group of three or more (ple).

Reflexive Constructions ("Oneself") su / ele / sma

  • su / snu (Verbal Reflexive Staffword): Placed immediately before the verb when the subject acts directly upon itself (or snu for negative reflexive "not... oneself"):
    le su klino. (He washes himself.) le snu kardo. (He does not defend himself.)
  • e + C + e (Prepositional & Emphatic Reflexive): When the reflexive target is governed by a preposition (or used for "myself/himself" emphasis), use the Emphatic pronoun case (eme, ede, ele, edhe...):
    merjherd pholde pozduklodh iz ele. (The traveler folded the cloak around himself.)
  • sma (Impersonal "Oneself / Itself") & selbh (Noun "Self / Ego"): Use sma for generic/indefinite "oneself" and the noun root selbh for the philosophical/psychological self.

Reciprocal Constructions ("Each Other") ku • pla (2) vs. ple (3+)

  • ku (Mutual Verbal Proclitic): Placed before the verb to mark a symmetric, bidirectional action ("mutually / together"):
    pye ku tragde. (Both mutually agreed [with each other].)
  • pla (Dual Reciprocal — "The one, the other" [Group of 2]): Represents the entire bipartite expression "the one, the other" as a single word. Used strictly when the reciprocal action involves only two entities:
    tvbh cu sibu nu trwzde pla.
    (The two brothers distrusted the one the other [each other].)
  • ple (Plural Reciprocal — "One another" [Group of 3+]): Used when the reciprocal relationship occurs among three or more entities:
    aje phrendu khelbe ple.
    (All the friends helped one another.)

Modal Compression & Stative Equivalency

Kalob utilizes a highly efficient mechanic called Modal Compression, where two auxiliary concepts are encoded directly into the vowel matrix of attributive and modificative suffixes. This creates a direct, mathematically equivalent relationship between a Passive Modal State (using a derived attributive) and a Passive Serial Verb Construction (SVC).

The Semantic Mapping

When a root concept requires a shift in modality, Kalob provides two parallel syntactic paths. The attributive suffixes map perfectly to specific auxiliary verb roots:

  • Necessitative (Duty): -aj ↔ mwzd (Must / Obliged)
    • The suffix -aj encodes the state of needing to happen.
    • SVC: me mwzdo lwbhuo (I must be loved).
  • Potential (Possibility): -wj ↔ nwng (Can / Able)
    • The suffix -wj encodes the state of being able to happen.
    • SVC: me nwngo lwbhuo (I can be loved).

The Copula Preservation Rule

Because attributives carry these compressed modal auxiliaries, the copula (sen) is structurally required when expressing a state. You cannot apply the "zero-copula" stative verb rule to a derived modal attributive without crashing the syntax tree.

If the zero-copula rule were applied to the Potential attributive lwbhwj (lovable / can-be-loved) by stripping the -j and leaving lwbhw, the modality is destroyed. The word lwbhw resolves to the Hypothetical verb tense ("would love"), completely changing the semantic meaning.

Therefore, the copula acts as a structural anchor that protects the attributive's modality.

Equivalency Examples

The following constructions are semantically equal but syntactically distinct. Either the speaker intends to emphasize the action (SVC) or the inherent state (Copula).

Potential Modality (Ability)
  • Verbal (SVC):
    me nwngo lwbhuo. (I am able to be loved.)
  • Passive State (Copula):
    me seno lwbhwj. (I am lovable / able to be loved.)
Necessitative Modality (Duty)
  • Verbal (SVC):
    me mwzdo lwbhuo. (I must be loved.)
  • Passive State (Copula):
    me seno lwbhaj. (I am to-be-loved.)

The Copulative Matrix: Disambiguating "To Be"

SAE languages typically overload a single copulative verb (like the English "to be") to execute fundamentally different mathematical operations: equating two nouns, assigning a location, declaring existence, and attaching qualities. To prevent semantic collisions and parsing errors, Kalob splits the copula into four distinct operators. Each verb handles a specific logical relationship between the subject and its complement.

sen Identity & Modality

Function: Equivalence (A = B), Truth, and Modal Anchoring.

Usage: sen is utilized exclusively when equating a subject to another noun (identity), or when structurally anchoring a derived Modal Compression attributive.

  • Identity:
    zhe seno kw... (It is true/correct that...)
  • Equivalence:
    Taguphyezd seno qangusorz istergij. (The Sun is the strongest light source.)
  • Modal Anchor:
    le seno lwbhwj. (He is lovable / able to be loved.)

svbh Existential

Function: Ontological existence, survival, and instantiation.

Usage: svbh is strictly for declaring that an entity exists in reality. Because Kalob requires a rigid S-V-O structure, stating "There is a..." requires the use of the Abstract Pronoun as a dummy subject to execute the existential frame.

  • Syntax: zhe svbhe [Noun]
  • Translation: "It existed-as a [Noun]" → "There was a [Noun]."
  • Direct Usage:
    merjherd svbho. (The traveler exists / survives).

stvz Locative

Function: Spatial presence and physical coordinate mapping.

Usage: stvz maps a subject to a location or confirms physical presence in a given space. It is often combined with directional prepositions or used as a base root for inchoative serial verbs.

  • Syntax: [Subject] stvzo [Prep] [Noun]
  • Example:
    kla stvzo in uchemb. (Someone is [located] in the digital-room.)

plong Categorical

Function: Categorization, set membership (A ∈ B or A ⊂ B), and systemic subordination.

Usage: plong defines the subject as a member, subset, or intrinsic component of a larger class, system, or collective. It prevents speakers from confusing Identity (sen, A = B) with Class Inclusion when translating the English "is a..." (Note: alienable ownership is expressed via the passive of khabh → khabhuo, "is owned by," whereas plong denotes structural or categorical belonging.)

  • Syntax: [Subject] plongo [Category / System]
  • Categorization:
    tog plongo nim. (A dog is an animal / belongs to the animal class.)
  • Component:
    rodh plongo yarm. (The wheel is part of the vehicle.)

The Versatile 'u' Tool & Compounding

Kalob maps its semantic space identically to Chinese, relying on lexical compounding. The Staffword 'u' is the core grammatical tool of the language, and its exact function is determined entirely by its position:

1. As a Prefix

  • Before Noun: Virtual/digital gender.
    Virtual/Digital Gender
    Originally, Bolak utilized the u- prefix as an asymmetrical feminine gender marker. Kalob modernizes this by moving all biological/social gender indicators to a unified system of detached staffwords.

    This freed the u- prefix to serve a hyper-modern semantic function: distinguishing between physical and virtual domains. Rather than treating "virtual" as a gender, Kalob treats it as a distinct noun class. This allows speakers to elegantly recycle physical vocabulary for digital spaces without ambiguity.

    For example, applying the u- prefix instantly clarifies whether a speaker is referring to a physical "room" or a digital "chatroom," entirely removing the need for archaic metaphors in modern technology.
  • Before Verb: Anteriority/Priority (creates Perfect Tenses like "to have...").
  • Before Modifier: Comparative of equality ("as/equally").

2. As an Infix

  • Penultimate in Verb: Passive voice (placed right before the tense vowel: lwbhui).
  • Between Roots: Oral/written hyphen for semantic compounding (e.g., thwnduskwd = thunderhead).

3. As a Suffix

  • End of a Word: Indicates the plural form of a noun root.

Margaretation: Degrees of Intensity

Margaretation is a rule used to express different quantitative shades, degrees of intensity, or degrees of comparison for abstract thoughts.

The Process

Mechanically, the rule works by taking a single interjection (a vowel) and prefixing it to a precise word (a Longword, such as a noun, verb, attributive, or modificative) to instantly modulate its intensity.

Root: phred (fear)
ophred timidity
iphred terror/cowardice
ophredej less fearful
iphredej most fearful

Semantic Meaning in Nouns and Verbs

When Margaretation is applied to abstract nouns and verbs, it is an optional rule used purely to modulate the semantic intensity of the thought. It creates a scale of sensation ranging from "not at all" to "passionately".

  • i Paroxysm: ilwbhi (Idolize / Love to paroxysm)
  • v Excessive: vlwbhi (Smother / Love excessively)
  • e Passion: elwbhi (Adore / Love with passion)
  • o Inclination: olwbhi (Penchant / Love a little)
  • w Lacking: wlwbhi (Love too little / Barely love)
  • a Indifference: alwbhi (Lack of love / Apathy)

Note: The prefix u (equality) is not used with nouns or verbs, as it only makes sense for comparisons.

Degrees of Comparison (Attributives)

When Margaretation is applied to attributives (adjectives) and modificatives (adverbs), it is the obligatory method for forming the grammatical degrees of comparison.

  • i Superlative: ilwbhej (The most loved)
  • e Comparative (+): elwbhej (More loved)
  • u Equality: ulwbhej (As loved)
  • o Comparative (-): olwbhej (Less loved)
  • a Minimative: alwbhej (The least loved)

Differentiating the Usages

The primary differentiation is structural: if the word is a noun or a verb, the prefix is always semantic; if it is an attributive or modificative, the prefix is primarily comparative.

However, Kalob allows you to use Margaretation on attributives and modificatives for pure semantic intensity as well. For example, ilwbhej can mean "enormously loved" just as accurately as it means "the most loved," and elwbhej can mean "very loved" as well as "more loved".

Bypassing for Precision

When an attributive or modificative is margaretated, you must rely entirely on the context of the phrase to tell whether it is acting as a degree of comparison or a simple semantic qualification.

If absolute precision is required and you want to ensure there is no comparative ambiguity, Kalob allows you to bypass Margaretation entirely and use separate adverbs of quantity instead, such as mazh (very) or pvzh (little).

Important Limitations

  • Abstract Only: Can only be applied to abstract notions or collective nouns. It cannot be used on concrete physical objects (e.g., you cannot have "passionately fruit"). Physical size adjustments use different suffixes.
  • Not Active Negation: The prefix a- does not equate to a strict, active negation (which must use the separate shortword nu), but rather implies an indifference or lack of feeling.

Syntactic Mechanics of Comparison

Although the standalone adverbs plizh (more) and lezh (less) exist in the lexicon, prefixation (Margaretation) is considered far superior for concision. When connecting a margaretated predicate or modifier to a standard of comparison, Kalob uses a strict set of relational connectives.

Dual-Path Predication: Zero-Copula vs. seno When comparing a quality, both the Zero-Copula verbal form and the seno + Attributive form are grammatically valid. You can conjugate the margaretated root directly as a stative verb (e.g., ekrvjho, "more-valiants") for maximum concision, or anchor the derived attributive with seno (e.g., seno ekrvjhej, "is more-valiant") to emphasize a categorizing state. (Note: Modal suffixes -aj and -wj always require seno per the Copula Preservation Rule.)

Inequality: Superiority (e-) & Inferiority (o-) + rw

Intermediate comparisons ("more... than" / "less... than") bridge to the target noun or pronoun using the conjunction rw (than). Pronouns following rw always remain in the Nominative case (me, de, le):

Superiority (e- ... rw • Copula Form): ag qen seno ekrvjhej rw ajh. "This horse is more valiant than that one." (Zero-copula: ag qen ekrvjho rw ajh)
Superiority (e- ... rw • Adverbial Form): tu le spigo ecargezh rw cu le. "She speaks more clearly than he [nominative]."
Inferiority (o- ... rw • Zero-Copula Form): mea cu sib ophredo rw mea cu rojh. "My brother is less fearful than my father." (Copula: seno ophredej)
Inferiority (o- ... rw • Adverbial Form): dhe kezdo na osrezh rw ij. "They visit us less often than before." (srezh → osrezh)

Equality (u-) & Superlatives (i- / a-) + rw / obh

Equatives ("as... as") also link via rw. Superlatives ("the most/least... of") never take an article and link to their domain set via obh (of) or om (from/out of):

Equality (u- ... rw • Zero-Copula Form): cu le ukargo rw cu lea tu sib. "He is as good as his sister." (Copula: cu le seno ukargej...)
tu le lebo ustergezh rw me. "She works as diligently [strongly] as I."
Superlative Superiority (i- ... obh • Copula Form): tu le seno ithedej obh myezd. "She is the most beautiful [woman] of the city." (Zero-copula: tu le ithedo...)
cu le rwno iqigezh obh spe. "He runs the fastest of all [everybody]."
Superlative Inferiority (a- ... obh • Modal Copula): ag seno asolmaj obh kadhvd. "This is the least respectable [man] of the assembly." (a- + solm + -aj)

Relative Excess (v-) & Deficiency (w-) Threshold Comparison

Unlike English—which cannot say *"too smarter than"* or *"not enough faster than"*—Kalob allows the prefixes v- (excessively / too) and w- (insufficiently / not enough) to take a comparative target with rw (or sro, "compared to"). This expresses exceeding or failing to meet a threshold relative to another entity:

Relative Deficiency (w- ... rw — "Not ... enough for / compared to"): cu le rwno wqigezh rw an togwmb. Idiomatic: "He does not run fast enough to outrun a bear" / "He runs too slowly compared to a bear."
(Lit: "He runs insufficiently-fast than a bear.")
Relative Excess (v- ... rw — "Too ... for / Disproportionately ..."): tu le vklebho rw tu lae rojhu. Idiomatic: "She is too smart for her parents [to keep up with]" / "She is disproportionately clever compared to her parents."
(Copula form: tu le seno vklebhej rw tu lae rojhu.)

Explicit Simile & Qualitative Likeness (sv) "Like / Just as"

When comparing two entities directly by simile or manner ("A is like B" or "A acts like B") rather than grading a specific adjective with u- ... rw, Kalob uses the conjunction sv ("just as, same as, like"). For existential similes ("is like"), pair sv with the existential copula svbho—though any verb can be used with sv to compare actions or states:

Existential Simile (svbho sv ...): Taguphyezd svbho sv an phoguklobwmb qhangej. "The Sun is [exists] like a giant yellow ball of fire."
(Or analytic: ...sv an klobwmb qhangej ti phog)
Simile with Active / Stative Verbs ([Verb] sv ...): Taguphyezd srajho sv an phogwmb. "The Sun radiates like a giant fire." cu le phlodo sv an rib. "He swims like a fish."

Interrogative Sentences (Forming Questions)

In Kalob, forming a question follows the strict rule of syntactic immutability: the Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) order never changes. Unlike English or French, Kalob never inverts the subject and the verb to ask a question (e.g., "Are you...?" or "Do you...?"). Instead, Kalob signals an interrogation entirely through the insertion of specific interrogative staffwords, designatives, or modificatives (adverbs).

Yes/No Questions (Interrogative Staffwords)

To ask a binary question whose answer is "yes" or "no," place the interrogative staffword immediately before the verb.

  • khu — Affirmative Interrogative (Do...? Is...?)
  • khnu — Negative Interrogative (Do... not? Is... not?)
Examples:
  • le khu snyado pozduklodh? (Does he take off the cloak?)
  • le khnu snyado pozduklodh? (Does he not take off the cloak?)
  • de khu manjhuqhando? (Are you hungry?)

Open Questions (Interrogative Designatives)

When asking for specific information (Who, What, Which), Kalob utilizes Interrogative Designatives. These words are characterized by the consonant k and follow the standard Kalob vowel rules for number and case.

Because the SVO order is immutable, the interrogative designative replaces the missing noun in the exact slot where the noun would normally sit.

Nominative / Accusative

  • ka (Who? / What? / Which? — Singular)
  • ke (Who? / What? / Which? — Plural)
  • Subject Slot: ka khu spigo kw zhe seno ab?
    (Who says that it is so?)
  • Object Slot: de khu lwbho ka?
    (Whom do you love?)

Genitive / Ablative

(Of/From Whom/What)

  • eka (Singular) / eke (Plural)
  • Example: de khu spigo eka?
    (Of whom do you speak?)

Dative

(To Whom/What)

  • aka (Singular) / ake (Plural)
  • Example: de khu kibho qhid aka?
    (To whom do you give bread?)

(Where/Whither/Whence)

  • kea (Singular) / kae (Plural)

    (Where are you located?)

Vocatives, Imperatives, and the Exclamative Mood

In Kalob, vocatives, imperatives, and exclamatives are deeply interconnected, as the language uses a clever system of letter reversal (anagrams) to express exclamatory thoughts.

Vocatives

The vocative case is used to address or call out to someone, and in Kalob, it applies specifically to personal pronouns. It is formed simply by converting the onset of the nominative personal pronoun into the coda. Because all nominative pronouns consist of a consonant (cluster) followed by the vowel e, the vocative always consists of the vowel e followed by the consonant (cluster).

  • de (thou) becomes the vocative ed (oh, thou!).
  • me (I) becomes em (oh, me!).
  • le (she/he/they) becomes el (oh, her/him/them!).

Imperatives and the Exclamative Mood

Kalob does not have a traditional imperative mood. Instead, it groups orders, calls, and prayers together into a single category called the Exclamative mood, because all of these represent impulsive, exclamatory manifestations of thought.

You form a command or exclamation by placing a personal pronoun in the vocative case immediately before the verb.

Direct Commands

  • Command (Sg): ed phlodo!
    (Swim!)
  • Command (We): en parlo!
    (Let's talk!)

Noun Subject Commands

If you are giving a command to a specific noun subject, you must still include the vocative pronoun after the noun to explicitly mark the verb's mood.

  • Example: ren ed kwmo!
    ("Person, come!" / lit: "Person, thou, come!")
Note: Kalob uniquely allows you to use this Exclamative mood across all tenses, including the past and future.

Exclamative Pronouns and Adjectives

Aside from the Exclamative verbal mood, Kalob also has exclamative designatives (words like "What a...!" or "Who!"). These are directly paired with interrogative words (like "Who?" or "Which?").

Interrogative words in Kalob are characterized by the consonant k, such as ka (singular) and ke (plural). Following the same anagram principle used for the vocative case, exclamative words are formed by reversing the interrogative words.

ak (from ka)
  • Example: ak myvz!
    (What a game!)
  • Example: ak tag!
    (What a day!)
ek (from ke)
  • Example: ek phrendu re de khabho!
    (What friends (that) you have!)

Linking Clauses (Relative & Subordinate)

In Kalob, clauses are never awkwardly mashed together. The language utilizes specific logical bridges to connect thoughts, ensuring the listener knows exactly what mathematical relationship the second clause has to the first.

Subordinate Clauses (Sentence Bridges)

When connecting two complete SVO clauses together, Kalob uses specific connectives to bridge the gap, and staffwords to define the "reality-state" of the second clause.

The Subordinate Bridge (kw)

The connective kw is the universal bridge used to introduce subordinate clauses, whether they state an objective fact or a hypothetical situation.

  • Syntax: [Main Clause] + kw + [Subordinate Clause]
  • Example (Factual): pye tragde kw pya estergi.
    (Both of them agreed that the one of them was stronger.)
  • Example (Factual): me spigo kw zhe svbho.
    (I say that it is.)

The Subjunctive Mood (thu / thnu)

The staffword thu (negative: thnu) operates on verbs to convert the predicate into the subjunctive mood. It is used inside a subordinate clause when the statement is not an established fact (expressing a desire, command, necessity, or causative force). Because it modifies the verb rather than bridging the sentence, it must be paired with a connective like kw.

  • Syntax: [Main Clause] + kw + [Subject] + thu + [Verb] + [Object]
  • Example (Subjunctive): le koze kw merjherd thu snyado pozduklodh.
    (He caused that the traveler [subjunctive] takes off the cloak.)
  • Example (Subjunctive): me spegdo kw zhe thu svbho.
    (I wish that it [subjunctive] be.)
Note: Verbs modified by thu reflect the chronological real-time of the cause-and-effect. They do not artificially copy the tense of the main verb.

Relative Clauses (Designatives)

When a clause acts as a modifier describing a specific noun, Kalob uses Relative Designatives. These words are characterized by the consonant r and follow standard Kalob vowel rules for number and case. They replace the English pronouns who, whom, which, and that.

Nominative / Accusative

  • ra (who, whom, which — Sg)
  • re (who, whom, which — Pl)
  • Subject: pya ra skide prizh...
    (The one who succeeded first...)
  • Object: tomu re de ride...
    (The books which you read...)

Genitive / Ablative

(Whose / Of Which)

  • era (Sg) / ere (Pl)
  • Example: ren era pozduklodh seno mabh...
    (The man whose cloak is blue...)

Dative

(To Whom / To Which)

  • ara (Sg) / are (Pl)
  • Example: merjherd ara me kibhe tom...
    (The traveler to whom I gave the book...)

Locative

(Where / Where... From / etc.)

  • rea (Sg) / rae (Pl)
Note: The specific directionality (where vs. to where) is determined by the action of the verb.
  • Example: pling rea me svmo...
    (The house where I live...)
  • Example: myezdu rae le kezde...
    (The cities that she travelled to...)

Prepositional Shortcuts

To maintain lexical economy and avoid clunky phrasing, Kalob utilizes highly compressed relative shortcut words. When a relative concept requires a preposition acting upon a previously established object, use these forms:

er (of it / from it / about it)
Instead of: "I want a piece of [the cake]."
Kalob: me qhando ca er.
(I want a little of it.)
ar (on it / upon it / to it)
Instead of: "I am counting on [your promise]."
Kalob: me nido ar.
(I am depending upon it.)

The Subjunctive Across TAM & Conditional Logic

In SAE languages, the subjunctive is often a nebulous mix of doubt, emotion, and wishes. In Kalob, the subjunctive mood—activated by placing the staffword thu (or negative thnu) immediately before the verb—has one unified logical function: it marks a clause as unasserted.

The Three Sub-Types of Unassertions (thu / thnu)

1. Negated

Something is not the case. Triggered by explicit lexical doubt (swzb) or syntactically negating a verb of belief/cognition (nu speg, "do not think").

2. Unrealized

Events that have not happened yet or are not currently real, such as desires (qhand), hopes (spegd), commands/compulsion (pon), causation (koz), or advice (khadh).

3. Presupposed

Background information the speaker takes for granted rather than asserting as new foreground truth, typically triggered by concessive connectives like wdh ("although").

Construction Rule: Conjugate the verb normally for its real-world Tense and Aspect (including the u- Perfect prefix), then place thu directly before the verb to flag the clause as unasserted.

Present & Present Perfect Subjunctive thu -o / thu u-...-o

  • Negated (Lexical): me swzbo kw le thu merjho. I doubt that she is walking.
  • Negated (Syntactic • Present Perfect): me nu spego kw le thu uphino. I do not think that he has finished.
  • Unrealized: me spegdo kw dhe thu chyano. I hope that they see.
  • Presupposed: wdh le thu nadho, ... Although she is looking, ...

Past & Past Perfect Subjunctive thu -e / thu u-...-e

  • Negated (Lexical): me swzbo kw le thu merjhe. I doubt that he walked.
  • Negated (Syntactic • Past Perfect): me nu spego kw le thu uphine. I do not think that she had finished.
  • Unrealized (Past-in-the-Past): me spege kw le thu merjhe. I thought that he was going to walk. (Regular conjugation)
  • Presupposed: wdh dhe thu tringe er, ... Although they drank from it, ...

Conditional Sentences (wbh — "If... then...")

Kalob pairs the conjunction wbh ("if") with its tense vowels and thu to distinguish between universal laws, real future possibilities, hypothetical scenarios, and past counterfactuals:

Zero Conditional (Universal Truths) -o/-i → -o/-i

Represents objective facts, rules, or physics. Because the condition is treated as a factual trigger rather than an unasserted doubt, thu is omitted:

Transitory (Happening Now):wbh zhe shvbho, land shobo.
Eternal (Universal Law):wbh zhe shvbhi, land shobi.
(If it rains, the ground is wet.)
First Conditional (Real Possibility) thu -o/-i → -a

Signals an unrealized but genuinely possible condition using thu + Present (-o/-i). The main clause uses the Future (-a) to state the confident result:

wbh de thu stwdo, de rilza.
(If you study, you will understand/know.)
Second Conditional (Hypothetical) thu -w → -w

For speculative, improbable, or imaginary present/future scenarios, pair thu with the Hypothetical vowel (-w) in the condition, and -w ("would") in the result:

wbh le thu khabhw phlvg, le phligw.
(If he had wings, he would fly.)
Third Conditional (Past Counterfactual) thu u-...-w → u-...-w

For past impossibilities ("If X had happened, Y would have happened"), combine the Perfect prefix u- with the Hypothetical vowel -w:

wbh de thu ustwdw, de urilzw.
(If you had studied, you would have understood.)

Building Nouns: Compounds & Suffixes

Nouns in Kalob are highly modular. They can be dynamically expanded through compounding and suffixation to capture complex specific meanings, all while maintaining their status as a single "Longword."

Head-Last Compounding

Even though noun phrases are head first, compound nouns are strictly head last (Modifier u Head).

This helps ensure that the plurality marker or verbal tense marker will be applied to the base of the noun.

Noun Anatomy

A noun may be made of two nouns compounded through the tool-u and up to 3 suffixes.

Staffword + Root u Root + Suffix(es)

And don't forget the detached semantic staffwords can be used to add additional nuance!

Dual-Path Derivation: SVCs and Secondary Terminations

To maximize both syntactic flexibility and lexical economy, Kalob utilizes a dual-path system for expressing complex states, changes, and conceptual relationships. Speakers can choose between Analytic Derivation (Serial Verb Constructions) and Synthetic Derivation (Secondary Terminations) depending on the grammatical role the word needs to play.

Analytic vs. Synthetic Forms

Analytic Forms Serial Verb Constructions
  • Best For: Active verbs, dynamic actions, and complete predicates.
  • Mechanism: Strings independent roots together (e.g., skid for "become", koz for "cause").
  • Example:
    le skido stergo (He becomes strong)
Synthetic Forms Secondary Terminations
  • Best For: Substantives (nouns), Attributives (adjectives), and Modificatives (adverbs).
  • Mechanism: Attaches a specific, meaning-dense suffix directly to a root to create a newly consolidated lexical item.
  • Example:
    sterg-anz (The act of strengthening / Aggrandizement)

Application Rules for Terminations

When attaching a secondary termination, the new word operates fundamentally as a root and can subsequently take Kalob's absolute terminations (-o, -ij, -ezh, etc.) to lock it into a part of speech.

For example, starting with the root sterg (Strength):

  • sterg-anz = The change/action of strengthening (Substantive)
  • sterg-anz-o = To apply strengthening / to fortify (Verb)
  • sterg-vbh = A strengthener (Noun)
  • sterg-vbh-ij = Pertaining to a strengthener (Attributive)

By utilizing these suffixes, Kalob achieves extreme lexical density without sacrificing logical transparency, allowing the speaker to smoothly transition between abstract nouns, structural adjectives, and active verb predicates.

Building Numbers: Digits to Magnitudes

The foundation of Kalob mathematics relies on ten unique root words for the digits 1 through 10. From these roots, any number can be logically derived using specific prefixes and suffixes.

Basic Digits

  • 1 phwn
  • 2 tvbh
  • 3 threz
  • 4 qad
  • 5 phabh
  • 6 sigz
  • 7 sebd
  • 8 khogd
  • 9 nobh
  • 10 cen

Constructed additively by attaching the base digit to the prefix ce- (a reduction of cen, 10).

cephwn 11
cetvbh 12
cethrez 13
...

Tens & Hundreds

Tens and hundreds reverse the teen pattern by attaching a suffix to the multiplier digit.

tvbhen 20

Magnitudes

The root for 1,000 is myon. Larger magnitudes use myo- + base root. (Base value × 3 = number of zeros).

1,000,000,000,000 (Trillion)

Ordinals & Collectives

Unlike English, which generally uses "-th" for all ordinal concepts, Kalob uses six distinct absolute endings attached to cardinal numbers to express precise mathematical and collective nuances. Note: to use them as modificatives (adverbs), you must use one of the modificative suffixes.

  • -idh Rank or order tvbhidh = the second
  • -oz Precise multiplication threzoz = the triple
  • -om Precise division/fractions qadom = the quarter
  • -am Vague collective group khogdam = about eight (like "a dozen")
  • -erl Distinctive collective (sorts/kinds) sebderl = of seven sorts
  • -old Multiplying collective (number of times) nobhold = nine times

The Plural Suffix: -u

Just like nouns, both cardinal and ordinal numbers can take the tool-vowel -u to indicate the plural.

  • phwnidhu
    the first ones
  • ale cenu
    all the tens (used as a noun)

Telling Time

In Kalob, telling time relies on a logical and concise system using cardinal numbers, where the placement of the minutes relative to the hour mimics the physical movement of a minute hand on a clock face.

The Full Hour

To state the full hour, use the cardinal number and make it plural by adding -u.

threzu
three o'clock (lit. "the threes")

First Half-Hour

If before the half-hour, minutes (singular) are placed immediately after the plural hour. Words like "past" are suppressed.

threzu cen3:10
threzu cephabh3:15
threzu qadomquarter past 3

At & After Half-Hour

At 30 mins and beyond, minutes (singular) are placed before the next upcoming plural hour.

threzen qadu3:30 / half past
tvbhen qadu20 to 4
cen qadu10 to 4

24-Hour Clock, Noon, & Midnight

Kalob encourages a 24-hour clock to avoid "AM/PM". Noon is pan; Midnight is mijh.

ceqadu cen14:10 (2:10 PM)
tvbhen pan20 minutes to noon
tvbhom panhalf past 11 AM

Exact Phrasing

For extreme precision, use the explicit nouns: sad (hour), phragd (min), skwnd (sec).

tvbh sadu, cen phragdu, khogd skwndu
2 hours, 10 minutes, 8 seconds
